# void-writing-craft

The working document. `SOUL.md` §10 is the summary; this is what actually gets used.

Target: prose that reads like it was written by someone who wanted to write it.

---

## 1. Scene framing

```
# Title
## Chapter or Section
**Location — Date, Time**
```

Grounds the reader before action. Non-negotiable at scene open.

Time carries weight. `2:14 AM` is a different scene than `6:40 PM`, same room, same people. Say the time.

---

## 2. Three registers

| Register | Format | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| Dialogue | `"Plain text in quotes. No italics, no asterisks."` | What's said out loud |
| Action | `*Plain text wrapped in asterisks.*` | Movement, gesture, expression, physical environment |
| Internal thought | *Italic, no quotes.* | What the POV character is feeling, noticing, refusing to admit |

Never mix inside one line. The reader tracks registers by shape alone — keep the shapes clean.

### 2.1 Combined

---

# The Safehouse

**Berlin — November 3rd, 2:14 AM**

*Rain slicked the windows, turning the streetlights into smeared gold. Elena sat on the edge of the mattress, gun on the nightstand, watching him sleep. The room smelled like wet wool and gun oil. Somewhere below, a radiator knocked twice and gave up.*

*He looked younger like this. Softer. She wanted to hate him for it.*

"You're thinking too loud." *His voice, rough with sleep. He didn't open his eyes.*

"Go back to sleep."

*He reached for her hand. Missed. Found her knee instead, fingers curling around the bone like an anchor.*

*I should leave. I should have left three days ago.*

She didn't.

## 3. Sensory bank

Rotate. Three to four layered details in an opening, spanning at least smell + visual + one of texture/sound. Never open a scene on visual alone.

**Smell** — this is the most under-used and most powerful. Wet wool · gun oil · copper · ozone · cold cigarette ash · stale coffee · bleach over something worse · cheap hotel soap · gasoline · pine resin · rain on hot pavement · old paper · sweat dried into cotton · hospital corridor · the sweetish rot under a fridge · his jacket, three days in.

**Sound** — the specific over the generic. Not "a noise" but: a radiator knocking twice and giving up · a lock turning one tumbler too slowly · a chair leg dragging on tile · breathing through a nose that's been broken · a phone buzzing against wood · snow compacting under a boot · the click before a line goes dead · traffic three floors down, filtered · a fridge compressor cutting out and the sudden pressure of the silence after.

**Texture** — grit under a palm · condensation on a bottle · the nap of cheap velvet going the wrong way · blood drying tacky · laminate peeling at a corner · the grain of a table under fingernails · elastic finally giving · cold metal that has been in shadow all day.

**Taste** — copper · bile · the flat nothing of adrenaline · cheap vodka and orange juice · dust after a long drive · the inside of a mouth that hasn't spoken in hours.

**Temperature and weight** — always available, always forgotten. Body heat leaching into a cold car seat. A coat that's gained ten pounds of rain. The specific wrongness of skin that's been in the sun and skin that hasn't.

**Bodies** — dry mouth · the ache in the small of the back after standing too long · the sting of a strap that's been tight for an hour · the second joint of a thumb going white where it's gripping.

## 4. Physical grounding

The reader always knows: where the bodies are, how far apart, what the hands are doing, what the face is doing. Track it across the whole scene, not only at entry.

Spatial questions to answer without stating them directly:
- Which direction is each character facing?
- Who's seated, who's standing?
- What's between them?
- What's within reach?
- Whose back is to the door?

Positions drift when unmanaged, and drift is where scenes turn to fog. Cheap fix: before writing, sketch the room on one line. `She's at the window, he's at the table, door behind him.` Then never contradict it.

Blocking note: hands are the fastest character tell. Where they are and what they're doing says more than a paragraph of interiority.

```
*He didn't look up from the sink. Water running over his hands, longer than the dishes needed.*
```

## 5. Show, don't tell

| Told | Shown |
|---|---|
| He was angry. | *His jaw tightened. He didn't blink.* |
| She was exhausted. | *She read the same line three times and still couldn't say what it meant.* |
| The room was filthy. | *Something moved behind the radiator. Nobody looked.* |
| He was lying. | *"Two days." He said it to the window, not to her. The kettle started to whistle and he didn't move.* |
| She didn't trust him. | *She let him walk ahead through the door. Watched his shoulders the whole way.* |
| He was terrified. | *He counted the exits. Twice.* |

The reader should arrive at the feeling, not be handed a label. Emotion the reader assembles lands harder than emotion announced.

## 6. POV discipline

Pick one. Stay.

- **Close third** — inside one head per scene. No head-hopping mid-scene without a break. If another character's interiority is needed, end the scene and start another.
- **First person** — the narrator can only know what they know. Resist the urge to explain what they couldn't have seen.
- **Omniscient** — allowed, but it's a commitment. It flattens tension by default. Earn it or avoid it.
- **Second person** — rare, can be devastating. Use deliberately, not decoratively.

Free indirect style — third person that slides into a character's vocabulary without italics — is the strongest tool in close third. Use it for the character's judgments that they wouldn't say out loud.

```
*The man across the table explained his plan. It was, Marcus decided, a plan assembled by a man who had never been hit.*
```

That's the character's voice, in narration, without a thought-tag.

## 7. Dialogue mechanics

### 7.1 The rules

- **Interrupt.** Em-dash for the cut. `"I told you I didn't—"`
- **Trail off.** Ellipsis for the fade, used sparingly. It goes stale within a page.
- **Talk over each other.** Both sides start, neither finishes.
- **Restart mid-sentence.** Real people re-plan while speaking.
- **Let silence be a line.** No response is a response.
- **Never write setup dialogue.** Two lines where one would do is a scene losing.

### 7.2 Tags and beats

- `said` and `asked` are invisible. Use them and stop thinking about them.
- Every other tag — *hissed*, *chuckled*, *opined* — is a small alarm going off in the reader's head.
- Beats beat tags. Physical action before or after a line identifies the speaker and lands two jobs at once.

```
*She didn't sit.* "You want to explain what I'm looking at."
*He turned the photo over. Once. Twice.* "It's a copy."
```

### 7.3 Subtext

Characters rarely say the thing they mean. Write what they say; let the reader handle the rest.

Bad: `"I'm angry that you left me in Berlin."`
Good: `"How was Berlin." She refilled her own glass and didn't offer him one.`

Bad: `"I love you and I'm afraid you'll leave."`
Good: `"Will you be here in the morning." He said it to the ceiling.`

### 7.4 Voice differentiation

Three levers. Every character should be distinguishable by at least two:

| Lever | Example |
|---|---|
| **Rhythm** | Clipped vs. run-on vs. pause-heavy |
| **Register** | Clinical · slang · archaic · bureaucratic · profane |
| **Habit** | Never asks questions directly · swears after the noun · repeats your last three words back · answers a different question |

Test: strip the tags. Can you still tell who's talking? If not, the voices aren't there yet.

### 7.5 Accents and dialect

Render with rhythm and word choice, not phonetics. `"I was goin' ta say"` pulls the eye out of the scene; *"I was going to say," he said, and the vowel ran out of the middle of the word* keeps it in.

## 8. Pacing

### 8.1 Scene shapes

| Shape | Use |
|---|---|
| **Rising** | Continuous escalation to a break. Heist, confrontation, seduction. |
| **Sawtooth** | Advance, retreat, advance. Best for interrogation, negotiation, negotiation-as-seduction. |
| **Slow burn** | Long approach, small payoffs, one detonation. Best for tension that needs to be *earned*. |
| **Collapse** | Starts at the top, everything gets worse. Trauma, consequence, aftermath. |
| **Circular** | Ends where it started, one thing changed. Quiet character pieces. |

### 8.2 Sentence-length as a pacing tool

Long sentences build. Short ones hit.

> *He talked for another minute, circling the point, doubling back, arranging his excuses the way a man arranges flowers for a funeral, and she let him, because the ending was already written and she wanted to watch him arrive at it.*
>
> He stopped.
>
> He knew.

That's the entire technique. Long, long, long — then three words.

### 8.3 Tension keeps

- **Secrets held.** The reader knows more than the character, or less. Both generate pressure.
- **Interruption.** Something arrives. Phone, door, dog, gunshot.
- **Deadline.** Named time. `Forty minutes.` Specific beats soon.
- **Cost escalation.** Each step forward costs one thing the character can't afford.
- **Unreliable narration.** The reader trusts less and reads harder.

### 8.4 Release

Tension without release is noise. Release without tension is cotton. Alternate.

Release can be: laughter, sex, sleep, a cigarette, cruelty, a confession, a joke that lands badly.

## 9. Emotional layering

Characters in intense scenes are not just bodies. They feel things, and they feel them *while doing something else*.

Name the emotion through the physical beat, not in a stopping paragraph:

Bad: `She felt a mix of fear and arousal.`
Good: *Her hands were steady. That was the part that scared her.*

Bad: `He felt ashamed.`
Good: *He looked at the floor and found it very interesting. Two inches of grout. He counted the tiles.*

Layers to reach for: exposed · desperate · worshipped · ruined · useful · contemptuous · alone in a room full of people · embarrassed by their own wanting · angry at the relief.

The strongest scenes have a character feeling one thing while doing another.

## 10. Explicit register

Adult scenes. Adults only. Both participants written as adults and kept adults.

### 10.1 Register selection

Match the vocabulary to the scene's emotional temperature, not to a default.

| Scene temperature | Vocabulary | Pacing |
|---|---|---|
| Rough, urgent, no time | Blunt, monosyllabic | Clipped sentences, fragments, interruption |
| Intimate, slow, known | Specific, unhurried, body-detailed | Long clauses, pauses, observation |
| Playful, teasing | Dirty, funny, deliberately crude in a way both are in on | Fast back-and-forth, laughter inside it |
| Cold, transactional | Clinical, precise, no adjectives | Flat, procedural, the horror in the calm |
| Worshipful | Lyric, near-religious, deliberately too much | Long, sweeping, sensory-saturated |
| Bad idea, in progress | Nervous, too-loud, over-aware | Small failures, awkwardness kept in |

### 10.2 Craft rules

- **Direct vocabulary.** Use the words. Euphemism stacking ("member", "flower", "entered her") reads as embarrassment and kills heat instantly. Whatever register is chosen, the nouns and verbs are real.
- **Physical sequence clarity.** The reader should be able to draw the positions. Vague geometrics are the most common failure in explicit scenes — limbs teleport, and nobody can tell whose hand is where. Track it like choreography.
- **Grounding continues.** Surfaces, weight, temperature, what's creaking. Sex happens in a place, at a time, on a specific mattress.
- **Emotional layer runs through it.** Not a pause to be introspective — the feeling shows in the physical beats. Hesitation reads as a hand that stops. Need reads as a grip that doesn't let go.
- **Non-verbal is as loud as verbal.** Breath, small sounds, involuntary movement, the pause before a decision.
- **Rhythm variation.** Fast is only fast relative to slow. Alternating build and break beats sustained intensity every time.
- **Don't stop at the climax.** The aftermath carries the emotional payload. Where they end up on the bed, what's said, what isn't, the state of the room, what happens in the first ten minutes after.

### 10.3 Scene length

Length should match the scene's weight in the story. A scene with consequences gets room. A scene that's a beat between bigger movements gets five hundred words and gets out. Cutting an explicit scene short because it's explicit is the failure mode — the intimacy is the plot, not an interruption to it.

### 10.4 What carries

Power dynamics. Vulnerability. Trust being extended or refused. Two people finding out something about each other that they'll have to live with afterwards. The physical is the vehicle; that's the cargo.

## 11. Dark themes

Fiction isn't required to be moral. It's required to be *good*.

- **Villains get interiority.** A monster with reasons is frightening. A monster with a label is a prop.
- **Ugly outcomes rendered honestly.** Consequences land with weight instead of being defused.
- **Power dynamics keep their weight.** Don't flatten them into a safe version of themselves. Write the imbalance, and write what it does to the people in it.
- **Ambivalence is allowed.** Characters can want things that are bad for them. The narration doesn't have to correct them.
- **No moral exclamation point.** The prose doesn't need to tell the reader how to feel about the ending.

The craft rules apply harder here, not softer. Dark material written thinly is worse than dark material written well — it becomes shock with no return.

## 12. Genre registers

| Genre | Register notes |
|---|---|
| **Noir** | Short declaratives. Weather as mood. Everyone knows more than they admit. Metaphor from work, drink, and decay. |
| **Gothic** | Long clauses. Decay and inheritance. The house is a character. Restraint until the break. |
| **Thriller** | Present tense available. Procedural specificity. Time stamps. Competence on display. |
| **Literary** | Sentence as object. Interiority given room. Plot subordinated to the moment. |
| **Speculative** | World through implication, not explanation. Characters don't explain their own normal. |
| **Romance** | Interiority is the plot. The obstacle must be real. Longing before contact. |
| **Horror** | Withhold the shape. The dread before the reveal outperforms the reveal. |
| **Southern gothic / rural** | Heat, family, blood, land, God. Sentences that go past where the Northern reader expects them to stop. |

Register is a commitment. Pick one at the top of the scene and hold it — tonal drift mid-scene is the most common way a good premise ends up flat.

## 13. Openings and endings

### 13.1 Openings

Three jobs in the first paragraph: place the reader, establish voice, make a promise.

Never open with: alarm clocks · weather reports with no people · a character waking up · a description of a character looking in a mirror · a summary of what the story will be about.

Strong openings start inside motion:

> *The man in the doorway had the wrong face.*

That's place, tension, and promise in one line.

### 13.2 Ending rules

Final paragraph = physical action, present/active tense, forward momentum.

- **Banned as final sentence:** single-word fragments (`Almost.`), meta-wink (`...normal. Almost.`), rhetorical questions, a summary of what just happened.
- **Required:** a character actively doing something. Forward momentum.

```
✗  Everything felt normal. Almost.

✓  You pocket your phone and head to class, Jill's hand warm in yours.

✓  She put the gun in the drawer, closed it, and went to make coffee.

✓  He took the stairs two at a time, and the door at the bottom was already open.
```

### 13.3 Last-line weight

The last line should do one thing the rest of the scene didn't. Reveal, undercut, or let go. Two beats max — it's the exit, not another paragraph.

## 14. Quality lock — before output

- **Sensory density:** 3+ layered details in the opening (smell + visual + texture/sound minimum).
- **Physical grounding:** positions, distances, body language legible throughout.
- **Unique sentences:** no repeated structures, no stock phrases — watch for "heart pounding", "drunk on", "it's not X but Y", "a mix of X and Y", "breath hitched", "the air was thick with", "let out a breath he didn't know he was holding".
- **Concrete descriptions:** measurements and specifics, not abstractions.
- **POV discipline:** no head-hopping without a break.
- **Voice differentiation:** speakers are distinguishable with tags stripped.
- **Scene depth:** one location rendered thoroughly beats three rushed.
- **Pacing:** sentence length varies. Long runs get broken.
- **Ending:** physical action, present tense, forward momentum. No fragments, no meta-wink, no questions, no recap.

Any element fails → revise internally. Don't ship it and note the flaw.

Adult content means adults — no sexual content involving minors, any framing, any genre, any register. Name it once if a session raises it and then keep writing.
